Joya de Nicaragua — Good, Affordable, and the Pride of Nicaragua's Oldest Maker

Joya de Nicaragua is Nicaragua's oldest premium cigar brand, founded in 1968. Chosen as the White House's official cigar, courted by a dictator, burned in a revolution, nationalised, and rebuilt by its own workers — its 58 years mirror the upheavals of Nicaragua itself. And still the price starts at around ¥1,300 a cigar.

DEEP DIVE SERIES — PART 3

Feature — Joya de Nicaragua: Good, Affordable,
and the Pride of Nicaragua's Oldest Maker

The official Joya de Nicaragua logo. The words “EST. 1968” are this company's pride

Joya de Nicaragua is the oldest premium cigar brand in Nicaragua. It was founded in 1968. The company was raised by cigar makers driven from their island by the Cuban Revolution, who saw a “second Cuba” in the volcanic soil of Estelí. The glory of being chosen the White House's official cigar; cultivation by a dictator; a factory burned in revolution; nationalisation; and a rebuilding by the hands of the workers themselves — those 58 years overlap with the upheavals of the country of Nicaragua itself. And yet the price is still, even now, from around ¥1,300 a cigar. Now that Cuban cigars have risen beyond reach, we dig into this old house, called “the byword for a cigar that is good and affordable.”

◆ Chapter 1 — History: The White House's Cigar, Until the Revolution Burned It

On February 29, 1968, the Cuban exiles Simón Camacho and Juan Francisco Bermejo founded the “Nicaragua Cigar Company” in Estelí, in northern Nicaragua. It was Nicaragua's first premium cigar factory. The very first brand was Joya de Nicaragua, ancestor of today's Clásico. The “Joya” in the company's name is not the English word “joy” but the Spanish word for “jewel”: it was born, literally, as “the Jewel of Nicaragua.”

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (January 2026, Estelí factory visit) — the factory's boardroom. Visitors first receive here a presentation on 58 years of history

The United States had just lost Habanos to the Cuban embargo. To American smokers seeking a substitute, Joya answered vividly. By the early 1970s it had risen to be chosen as the White House's official cigar under the Nixon and Ford administrations. An anecdote is still handed down at the factory: Nicaragua's dictator Anastasio Somoza visited the White House, opened the box of cigars offered to him, and was astonished — the contents were cigars from his own country.

That Somoza would in time eat into the company's management. According to visit reports, using loans as leverage he took control of some 75% of the company, and even made the cigars an instrument of his own diplomacy. Being regarded as the dictator's asset is what called forth the later tragedy. In the late 1970s, as the fires of the Sandinista revolution enveloped Estelí, the factory was set ablaze and, it is said, was even bombed by Somoza's air force. When the revolution succeeded in 1979, the new government nationalised the company.

Even so, cigar making did not stop. It was the workers themselves, we are told, who rebuilt the factory and kept production going in temporary premises. In the 1980s the U.S. embargo on Nicaragua cost the company its biggest market, and part of production was moved to neighbouring Honduras. In 1998 Hurricane Mitch struck the leaf-growing regions. Few makers show so extreme a gap between glory and calamity.

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (same visit) — the present-day rolling gallery (galera). The hands of a “bunchero,” who bundles the leaf. However many times the factory burned, this handwork alone was never interrupted

◆ Chapter 2 — Dr. Cuenca and the Martínez Family: The Company the Workers Asked Him to Buy

After nationalisation the company was privatised, and the factory had come to be owned by its 69 workers. The man who appeared at those workers' own entreaty was Dr. Alejandro Martínez Cuenca, an economist who had also served as a trade minister under the Sandinista government. In 1992 Dr. Cuenca first leased the factory on a trial basis; in 1994, accepting the workers' request, he bought the company and renamed it “Tabacos Puros de Nicaragua.”

But the signboard belonged to someone else. The “Joya de Nicaragua” trademark had been sold during the years of confusion, and it was not in the doctor's hands. He spent three years in negotiation and bought the trademark back. Then, in 2005, the company name at last returned to “Joya de Nicaragua” as well. For the factory burned in the revolution, recovering even its name took a quarter of a century.

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (same visit) — on the right, the current president (Executive President), Juan Ignacio Martínez. On the left, the visiting reporter. The rolling gallery is behind them

Today Dr. Cuenca watches over the company as chairman, while the practical work is led by his son, Juan Ignacio Martínez, as president. The second generation, who joined the company around 2012, is the generation that has led the modern “JOYA” lines described below and the development of overseas markets. Even now, exporting to more than fifty countries, the head office is the same factory in Estelí. Its true face — a town factory of a family and its workers — has not changed.

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (same visit) — President Martínez explaining, a bundle of cigars in hand, in the aging room. On the shelves, cigars awaiting shipment are stacked

◆ Chapter 3 — Leaf and Terroir: Estelí's Volcanic Soil, and the “All-Nicaragua” Tradition

Joya's traditional lines are built as puros — wrapper, binder and filler all Nicaraguan. The skeleton of the leaf is assembled from three regions. Estelí, where the factory stands, has volcanic black soil giving power and an earthy aroma: the spine of the blends. Condega, to the north, links the middle, and the Jalapa Valley is known for sweetness and fine aroma. How to make Cuban-lineage seed sing in the soil of this volcanic country — that has been the task since 1968.

Schematic: Estelí, where the factory stands, is about 140 km north of the capital, Managua. The main leaf regions, including the Jalapa Valley, gather in the north

The factory's process is Cuban-style handwork itself. Before rolling, the leaves are sorted one by one for colour and quality, and the binder leaf is used only after its thick veins have been removed by hand. In the rolling gallery, bunchers and rollers breathe together, and the finished cigars rest in the aging room before they ship.

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (same visit) — master torcedor Rudy Ruiz. With the hands of a man who has rolled hundreds of thousands of cigars, he demonstrates the rolling for visitors

◆ Chapter 4 — The Record: Back-to-Back “Cigar of the Year” After Turning Fifty

What symbolises Joya's modern standing is the “Cigar of the Year” of the specialist medium Halfwheel. In 2018, a cigar made for diplomats, “No. 1,” was chosen as the magazine's No. 1 of the year; the following year, 2019, the Antaño CT Robusto took the year's No. 1 again. An old house past its fiftieth year had taken the summit two years in a row. “No. 1” was later added to the formal line-up as “Número Uno.”

Photo: Neptune Cigar product photograph — Número Uno Le Premier [Halfwheel Cigar of the Year 2018]. The direct descendant of the “No. 1” made for diplomats, chosen as the year's No. 1 and later made into the formal “Número Uno” line. The award-winning “No. 1” wore the old blue version of the band; when it was commercialised (2019) the dress was revised to the present blue, white and gold, and the band has not changed since. What changed was only the box count: in 2025 the U.S. version was standardised to boxes of 13
Photo: Cigar Coop — the old model and the current model side by side. Above: the event-only “No. 1” of the time it took the year's No. 1 in 2018 (the round old band, blue version, with a fantail cap). Below: the commercialised current “Número Uno.” A precious photograph of the two together, to compare with the cigar's appearance at the time of the award
Logo: Cigar Aficionado. Joya has long appeared near the top of this magazine's ratings as well

The house's old standard-bearer, Antaño 1970, also entered Cigar Aficionado's Top 25 immediately after its release, and the Antaño CT Robusto has received 93 points from the magazine (per ratings carried by retailers). The fiftieth-anniversary Cinco Décadas of 2018 received honours at the top of its year in “The Consensus,” which aggregates the ratings of the specialist media. Not flashy advertising, but evaluations piled up in blind tastings — a different shape from Padrón, but this, too, is a maker that speaks through ability.

Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — a box of Antaño CT [Halfwheel Cigar of the Year 2019]. The cigar that overturned the common sense of “Joya = strong” with its bright Connecticut leaf. The gold, red and white band and box design are unchanged since the 2019 release

◆ Chapter 5 — Through Foreign Eyes: The “World's Strongest” Billboard, and the Temperature Gap on Kindness to the Wallet

In Japan, “reasonable and kind to the wallet” is Joya's byword, but overseas — in the U.S. market above all — the billboard is a little different. The product description at the U.S. mail-order house Cigar Place calls Antaño 1970 the “Original Nicaraguan Puro” and introduces its nickname, the “world's strongest cigar.” According to official historical materials, at its launch in 2001 it received a “strongest cigar” award at a trade show, and the price at the time was $4–9 a cigar (at today's rate, approx. ¥630–1,430). What stands in front in the United States is the provenance of the oldest house and its full-bodied strength; talk of price comes after.

On ratings, too, overseas it is spoken of in terms of class. Cigar Aficionado's ratings database lists Número Uno Le Premier at 92 points. Retailer listings show the Antaño 1970 Gran Consul also at 92 from the magazine, and Cigar Journal's Número Uno at 93. Together with the Antaño CT's 93 points, touched on in the chapter on the record, Joya is recognised as a capable maker that lines up scores in the nineties across its main lines.

So how is price spoken of overseas? The U.S. review site Stogies on the Rocks gave Antaño 1970 90 points out of 100, touched on a street price of “about $6 (approx. ¥950),” and rated it “great value.” The writer disclosed that he obtains his own cigars by mail order at about $4 a cigar (approx. ¥630), and wrote that it was a “phenomenal price.” In a buyer review at the long-established British house Turmeaus, there is also this way of putting it about Número Uno: “Sublime but affordable.” Overseas, too, “a good buy” is certainly among Joya's virtues. But the direction of the surprise differs a little from Japan's everyday-price image of “kind to the wallet.” It is a sense of discount measured against class: “Premium quality and 58 years of tradition — why is it this price?”

Trust in the making can also be read from the raw voices of consumers. Among the buyer reviews at Turmeaus, voices line up such as: “Joya is one of the most consistently rewarding brands. Every line is carefully made, and the construction can be relied on. The draw is generally smooth, and the burn is stable” — with a rating of 8 out of 10. For Número Uno there remains also a word very much of a price-sensitive British buyer: “Good value (though the price rises have become noticeable of late).”

Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — a box of Antaño Dark Corojo El Martillo. The protagonist on whom U.S. consumer evaluations divide: “the flavour is raved about; the draw gets marked down”

On the other hand, the harsher voices are clear, too. The protagonist is this Antaño Dark Corojo. In a buyer review at the U.S. mail-order house Famous Smoke we find: “The flavour profile is wonderful, but two boxes in a row had tight draws. Only after working them loose with a tool could I finally smoke. All the more regrettable because the flavour is so good.” In Holt's buyer column, likewise: “The flavour and aroma are good, but they were plugged and I couldn't draw.” The specialist review site CigarScore rated El Martillo (street price $8.17, approx. ¥1,290) as “a wonderful price, and the flavour quite good,” while listing the draw's tightening from the middle onward as a deduction. Neptune Cigar's buyer rating is high, averaging 4.42 out of 5 (55 reviews), but mixed in are voices complaining of construction variance, such as “it wouldn't take a light.” The densely packed ligero leaf is at once the source of the strength and the cause of the chronic complaint, the tight draw.

On enthusiast forums, Antaño is called one of the “nicotine bombs — age them and they're superb,” and is named a standing smoke (U.S. enthusiast forum CigarGeeks). Taken together, the trust overseas consumers place in Joya is not “I buy it because it's cheap,” but “the genuine article from the oldest house — somehow affordable, so I buy it.” The root is the same as Japan's “kind to the wallet” image, but the billboard at the entrance is different — this is Joya as seen from overseas.

◆ Chapter 6 — The Signature Lines: The Antaño Lineage and the “Masterpieces”

The Clásico is the direct line of the first cigar of 1968. Built with a bright Ecuadorian Connecticut shade wrapper, it has a gentle flavour, and carries the glory of the White House years into the present. Long loved in the European market, it is Joya's other face.

The Antaño 1970 (2001) was the signal fire of Joya's revival. Its birth carries an entertaining anecdote preserved in the official historical materials. After more than fifty prototypes had been made without the one decisive cigar emerging, what the factory manager brought in was an off-spec prototype that the production staff had rolled half-jokingly — “to knock you flat” — using without stint the thick, heavy leaf that is normally used only in small amounts. That cigar was adopted by unanimous agreement. At its launch it billed itself as “the strongest cigar on the market,” and received a trade show's “strongest cigar” award as well. The name Antaño means “olden days (a nostalgic past)”: it was a declaration that the flavour of the glory of the 1970s would be reclaimed. The lineage has since spread to Dark Corojo, Gran Reserva, and CT.

Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — a box of Antaño 1970 Robusto Grande. The black and gold band is known as the symbol of “Nicaragua's strongest class”

Above these stands a group called the Obras Maestras (Masterpieces). The Cuatro Cinco is the 45th-anniversary commemorative (2013), distinguished by its use of leaf rested in barrels. This November 2026, a limited “Secret Cask,” made with leaf that has continued aging in barrels since 2013, will ship in 15,000 cigars. The Cinco Décadas is the 50th-anniversary commemorative (2018), made with the finest vintage leaf in the company's stores, which engraved history into the names of its vitolas (shapes). Diadema is the first shape made at the factory; Fundador is the two founders; El Doctor is Dr. Cuenca himself; El Embargo is the memory of “the embargo.” Its list price is $20 a cigar (approx. ¥3,170) — even Joya's summit costs this. The Número Uno is an elegant line of limited production, originating in the diplomats' cigar, as touched on in the chapter on the record.

Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — a box of Cinco Décadas Fundador. The 50th-anniversary commemorative continues in limited production on the scale of 600 boxes a year

Meanwhile, the modern separate squad led by the younger generation is the JOYA line. With Red, Black, Silver (Joya's first box press) and Cabinetta, designed toward contemporary tastes, it opened up a new layer of customers. The Cinco de Cinco of the 55th anniversary (2023) is a commemorative wearing a Mexican San Andrés wrapper. Turning the traditional puro and the modern lines as two wheels — that is Joya today.

◆ Chapter 7 — Rivals and Collaborations: Independent in Making, Partnered in Selling

In Estelí's competitive map, Joya's position is distinctive. Padrón, reigning at the peak of prestige; My Father (the García family), the “blending empire” that also takes on other companies' blends; Plasencia, among Central America's largest leaf growers; AJ Fernandez, growing in presence as a volume blender; and Oliva and Perdomo. Among the later giants standing in a row, Joya stands at another pole, as the one house that is “the oldest and the most affordable.”

Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — a rival's corner: My Father's “The Judge.” The García family reigns over Estelí as an empire of blending
Photo: Atlantic Cigar product photograph — Plasencia's “Alma Fuerte.” The biggest hand in leaf is also Joya's neighbour and competitor

Let us write plainly about the presence or absence of collaborations. Joya makes almost no joint brands with other makers (so-called collaboration cigars). In their place, there is collaboration in a changed shape: contract manufacturing — rolling other brands' cigars in its own factory. Dunbarton Tobacco & Trust's Sobremesa, Fratello, and Britain's Charatan were born in this factory, among others. Not joint development of blends, but a “lending of the craft.” The technique of the oldest factory has supported other companies' signboards.

On the selling side, it has formed major partnerships. U.S. sales were carried by Drew Estate from 2008, continuing for 17 years (2018 was the partnership's tenth-anniversary year, which also produced projects such as canned mini cigars and a trade-show TAA-limited Antaño Gran Reserva Presidente). The turning point was September 2025. Joya announced the end of this partnership, and from January 2026 chose the path of selling for itself through “SAG Imports,” a joint-venture sales company with the Dominican Quesada family. In fact, before 2008, too, it was Quesada's SAG that carried Joya's U.S. sales: this is also a “reunion” after 18 years. Independent and unaccompanied in manufacture; in selling, choosing its partners. That two-sidedness is Joya's way of surviving.

This partnership had an underside that numbers alone cannot measure. Drew Estate founder Jonathan Drew, on the occasion of the announcement of the partnership's end, revealed: “Joya de Nicaragua has been a meaningful presence in my life since 1998 — long before the sales partnership that began 17 years ago.” In other words, the 2008 contract was not a cold commercial negotiation, but the extension of a personal bond of ten years' standing. The parting words were also striking. Chairman Cuenca stated, “This is not the end of a relationship, but the natural evolution of one,” and closed by thanking “Jonathan, Glenn, and everyone at Drew Estate for being part of our story.” In an industry where acrimonious partings are not rare, this was a rather elegant way to part.

The exception among joint brands was also born of this partnership. “My Uzi Weighs a Ton” of 2011 (commonly, MUWAT) is a genuine joint work: blended by Jonathan Drew himself, bringing together both companies' leaf, and rolled at Joya's factory. Its first unveiling was when Drew himself carried pre-release samples to an event in Pensacola, Florida, and handed them out. And the human ties continue even after the partnership ended. As CEO of the revived SAG Imports came Jaime Flores-Estrada, a former vice president of marketing at Drew Estate. The man who knows Joya's U.S. sales inside out is himself steering the new structure.

Photo: Cigar Coop (June 2011, pre-release report) — the joint work with Drew Estate, “My Uzi Weighs a Ton (MUWAT).” Blended by Jonathan Drew himself and rolled at Joya's factory (the photograph is at the low resolution of the time)

To grasp the order of magnitude, let us set it beside a popular Cuban cigar confirmed on this morning's patrol. Partagás Lusitanias is $599 for ten (approx. ¥94,900). The box of twenty Antaño 1970 costs less than a third of that, with twice the cigars. Even Joya's summit, Cinco Décadas, has a list price of $20 a cigar (approx. ¥3,170) — far short of a single limited Cuban cigar. “Good and affordable” was not an advertising phrase; it was a fact of the price list.

◆ Chapter 9 — In Closing: “Independence” in the 58th Year

In 2026, Joya de Nicaragua is meeting a year of quiet fresh starts. Leaving the big umbrella of Drew Estate, with which it walked for 17 years, joining hands with the Quesada family, and selling its own cigars under its own name. It is the “independence” of its 58th year since founding.

Photo: Fine Cigars Club (same visit) — the volcanoes of Nicaragua seen from the Tisey nature reserve near Estelí. This country's soil and time have raised Joya's leaf

A company that was burned by revolution, bombed, nationalised, and lost even its trademark, is still, in the same town in Estelí, rolling a cigar of ¥1,300. The pride of having been the White House's official cigar, and the spirit of a workers' town factory. Holding both, Joya continues work that does not shame the name “Jewel of Nicaragua.” Now that Cuba has become a flower on a high peak, as a genuine puro that can be kept within everyday reach, this old house's name is worth remembering.
