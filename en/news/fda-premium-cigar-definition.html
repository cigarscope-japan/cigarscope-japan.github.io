Court Keeps the 'Premium Cigar' Definition, So Flavored Cigars Stay Under FDA Regulation

A federal judge has upheld the eight-part 'premium cigar' definition used since 2020: handmade cigars meeting it stay outside the FDA's deeming rules, while flavored and machine-made cigars remain subject to product approval and user fees.

The cigar industry's decade-long lawsuit against the FDA may finally have ended on April 15, 2026. Judge Amit P. Mehta upheld the same definition of "premium cigar" that has been used since 2020, which means handmade cigars meeting it are not subject to the FDA's deeming regulations — product approval, user fees and other restrictions — while flavored cigars and machine-made cigars remain subject to those rules. In practical terms, the way cigars are regulated has not changed: yesterday's rules remain today's regulations.

A cigar qualifies only if it meets all eight requirements: it is wrapped in whole tobacco leaf; it contains a 100 percent leaf tobacco binder; at least 50 percent of the filler by weight is long filler; it is handmade or hand rolled with no machinery beyond simple tools; it has no filter, nontobacco tip or nontobacco mouthpiece; it has no characterizing flavor other than tobacco; it contains only tobacco, water and vegetable gum; and it weighs more than 6 pounds per 1,000 units. The definition was the one issue the appeals court sent back after the case — filed in July 2016 by the Cigar Association of America (CAA), Cigar Rights of America and the Premium Cigar Association, and effectively decided in August 2023 — was affirmed in January 2025.

The CAA was the only party that wanted a different definition, arguing for a shorter, broader one that would have opened the door for flavored cigars to qualify. Mehta dismissed those arguments, writing that if a definitional refinement is appropriate, "rulemaking, not judicial fiat, is the better way to achieve it." The ruling does not make the exemption permanent — the FDA could restart rulemaking — and one loose end remains: cigar companies paid more than $100 million in user fees, estimated at 5 to 10 cents per cigar, on cigars sold from August 2016 to 2023, and how that money might be returned is still undecided.
